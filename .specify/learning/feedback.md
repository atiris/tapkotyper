# Feedback

## What
- Correct key: instant soft visual and sound confirmation.
- Wrong key: the target letter gently wobbles, the expected key glows on the keyboard,
  and the narrator may give a short hint. Nothing is lost; the child simply types the right key.
- After three mistakes on one key, the narrator shows which key it is and which finger presses it;
  play goes on, with no extra practice in between.
- Results show accuracy first (stars for clean words), speed only as a personal trend
  ("faster than last week"), never compared with other children.
- A parent switch "no time pressure" turns every timed element into an untimed one.
- Rhythm and speed count only real moves between keys: pressing the same key again
  ("ffff") adds nothing to the rhythm gauge, the session's characters per minute or the
  day's reaction times. The gauge opens below its top, follows the real pace and sinks
  within a few seconds once typing slows down or stops.
- Own practice and the target range show a small accuracy ring for each key in focus next
  to the rhythm gauge (the key on the disc, a space or Shift as a sign). The ring uses a log
  scale of the errors, so 80 to 100 % fill most of it, and its color says how far the key is:
  red below 80 %, orange below 90 %, yellow above, green once the key is ready.

## Why
- Typing one key over and over in the middle of a story makes no sense to a child; the
  hint with the finger is enough, and own practice is there for drills.
- Holding or hammering one key faked speed: the gauge stayed high and parents read wrong
  speeds in the statistics.
- Accuracy between 80 and 100 % decides whether a key is ready; a plain ring would show
  85 % and 98 % almost alike, so the child would not see the small gains that open the next letter.
- Racing and rigid scoring push speed over accuracy and discourage children
  ([research](../../.research/features/accuracy-first-feedback.md)).
