# Lesson progression

## What
- Keys unlock gradually, starting from the home row (`f j` with the space bar, then `d k`,
  `s l`, `a` and `ô`/`;`), then the upper row, the lower row, then (Slovak) the diacritics
  track, and digits last.
- Space, period and comma are part of the letter path, not a track after it: space with the
  first pair, `.` after `r u`, `,` after `n` (US: `'` right after it), `?` after the last
  letter (Slovak `-` too, US `:`), Slovak `!` and `:` after the first diacritic `á`, US `!`
  and `-` with the digits.
- Capitals come in the middle, in two steps: the left Shift (capitals of right-hand letters)
  after `p`, the right Shift (left-hand letters) after `z` (US `y`). Each Shift is its own
  lesson key, measured on the capitals it serves. Capital accented letters appear only when
  the letter and its Shift are both known, and rarely.
- Space, punctuation and the Shifts get ready sooner than letters (fewer presses), so they do
  not hold the next letter back for long.
- A new key is introduced alone first (where it is, which finger), then mixed with known keys.
- Home row keys and the first reaches come in mirrored pairs (same finger, other hand).
  When the newest keys are typed accurately and steadily, the next ones unlock; a key
  counts as mastered at a stricter target (more presses), which fades its guide.
  The parent can override and pick the active keys by hand.
- Enter now and then: once the key under the right little finger on the home row (`ô`,
  US `;`) is unlocked, about one target in 20 (a word or a phrase) ends with an Enter mark `↵`
  (never two in a row, at most two per story level; own practice the same rate without a
  cap). After the letters the target waits for Enter. Not in drills, levels of single
  letters, the main gate's code or free typing; a phrase split into parts carries it on
  its last part. Enter presses count in the key
  statistics like any key, but Enter is no lesson key: it never holds the next keys back.
- Adaptive practice: keys with low accuracy or slow reaction appear more often in words.
- Story levels use the child's current key set, so the story and the lessons move together.
- Each story level needs a number of known keys that only grows: the dragon story starts
  gently (Honey Meadow 3, 3, 4, 5; Rocket 7, 8, 9, 10; New Planet 12, 13, 14, 15; Dragon
  Valley 16, 17, 17, 18; Egg 19, 19, 20, 20; a repeated count is a consolidation level with
  more words and no new key, and a super-obstacle asks what the world's last level asks),
  the robot story climbs evenly from 10 to 34 over its 25 levels, the fairy story from 20
  to 62 (capitals, diacritics and digits last), but never more than the profile's whole
  lesson path, so a child who knows it all plays everything. Keys come in groups, so a
  count between two groups (4 after `f j` and space) waits for the whole next group. All
  unlocked keys count (space, punctuation and Shifts too);
  with the parent's own key choice, its keys count. A level reached (the one before has its
  star) but still short of keys shows a key on the map; tapping it, the narrator says how many
  letters are missing. When keys of the newest group are not ready yet, the key's tooltip,
  the narrator and a story still closed for keys also name them ("type these more
  accurately: k, l"), and the narrator offers the [target range](../game/target-practice.md)
  once a world is done (that world's, else the last finished one), own practice before. "Continue" skips such levels: it replays
  the last open one, or starts own practice when none is open. Rescue missions and the
  super profile have no key need.
- Own practice mode: calm typing of words from the chosen keys, no story. Every way into
  it (the practice button, "Continue" with nothing open, the narrator's offer on the map) first
  opens a key picker; a task's practice, a drill from the stats and the parent's own text
  do not, they have their own targets. The picker shows the keyboard with the chosen keys
  lit in their finger colors, a slider over the lesson order (step 1 is `f j` and space,
  each step adds the next three keys, the last is the whole order) and opens with the
  child's active keys, on the smallest step that holds them. A click on a key adds or takes
  it away ("own choice"). own practice needs at least two letters. The parent's own words
  are not mixed in, since they bring other letters. Above the keyboard three buttons pick
  the way to type: Copy (default), Dictation (only with a voice for the language) and
  Remember it ([mechanics](../game/mechanics.md)); each has a tooltip, and one line under
  them says what the chosen one asks. Nothing is stored: the picker opens
  with the active keys and Copy each time.
- Just typing ([free typing](../game/free-typing.md)) has no key choice: every key of the
  layout may be typed and nothing of it counts in the statistics, so it never makes a key
  ready or opens new keys.

## Why
- New letters wait for accuracy, which a child cannot see; naming the letters and offering
  a fun way to train them turns a silent lock into a clear goal.
- Own practice is for practising exactly what the child (or parent) wants: the slider
  is quick, the lit keyboard shows what is in, single clicks fine-tune it. The way to type
  is a choice for one run, so it is not stored. Children do not know what "Dictation" or
  "Remember it" mean, so the line under the buttons says it in their words.
- Gradual unlocking with weak-key weighting (Keybr, Typing.com) is what users praise most
  for real progress ([research](../../.research/features/lesson-progression.md)).
- Space early is normal (Typing.com starts with "J, F and Space"); period and comma with the
  letters make even short targets read like text; capitals at the end of the path are the
  block every big course has, and Shift chords are hard for children, so each Shift comes
  alone at a middle point with plenty of known words
  ([research](../../.research/learning/space-punctuation-capitals.md)).
- Seven keys in the first world were too hard for a beginner, so the first world asks for
  three to five and the curve climbs from there; a small key need per level still keeps the
  words varied, and a child who knows more is not held back. The robot story opens with ten keys and asks for more, so it grows
  with the child instead of waiting for finished worlds; the fairy story opens with
  twenty, since words heard or remembered need more letters to be worth it.
- A typist presses Enter with the right little finger, so a little practice is worth it;
  it is a side key that comes rarely, so it must not decide when new letters open.
- Mirrored pairs keep the hands balanced, and a lighter unlock target keeps the first
  world from feeling like endless `f j` drills; mastery still asks for solid accuracy.
